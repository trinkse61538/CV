Why lesson records

A lesson should explain what changed in future behavior.

Projects often end with a list of observations, but observations alone do not show whether the pattern is reliable, transferable or still uncertain.

A lesson record connects a concise claim to the decision and outcomes that produced it. It distinguishes direct evidence from corroboration and inference, defines where the lesson applies, records exceptions and specifies when the conclusion should be reviewed.

The result is not a collection of motivational statements. It is a public-safe evidence layer designed to improve later decisions.

Lesson record schema

Every lesson uses the same eight-part evidence contract.

The schema prevents useful patterns from losing their origin, confidence or applicability limits.

01

Identity

ID, title, status, domain, owner, date and linked decision or project.

02

Claim

The reusable lesson stated as a concise, testable operating proposition.

03

Origin

The decision, project event, failure or outcome that created the learning opportunity.

04

Evidence

Direct observations, corroborating records, measurements and explicit source links.

05

Confidence

Current support level and which parts remain inference rather than observation.

06

Applicability

Situations where the lesson should guide work and conditions where it may not fit.

07

Action

The practical behavior, checklist or decision rule produced by the lesson.

08

Review

Counter-evidence, expiration triggers, review phase and links to future lessons.

Evidence ladder

Confidence must reflect the kind of support available.

A persuasive explanation is not automatically strong evidence. Each source is labeled by what it can actually support.

Evidence typed explicitly
LEVEL 01

Direct observation

A visible outcome, test result, published artifact or recorded event directly supports the claim.

Corroborating pattern

LEVEL 03

Reasoned inference
